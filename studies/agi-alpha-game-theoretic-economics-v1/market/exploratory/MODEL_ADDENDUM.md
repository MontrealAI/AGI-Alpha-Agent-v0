# Postprimary exploratory boundaries

The original 87-condition, 24-seed primary experiment is preserved in full. It produced no profitable enterprise and no capability adoption. Its frozen strong conventional/AI alternatives, low initial trust, integration cost and finite procurement pool prevented the selected enterprise from establishing a sufficiently profitable service. That result applies to the specified assumption envelope; it cannot identify the entire market.

This extension was designed **after seeing that primary result**. It is a bounded exploratory study, not a preregistered replication or a replacement result. The unmodified core now evaluates exactly 206 declared configurations with twelve new seeds. No quality premium, customer-effort reduction, novel capability or customer demand is assigned to the AGI ALPHA label.

The 192-cell grid crosses integration cost (USD0/10/50), weekly changed-input opportunity probability (.25/.5), unknown provider finalization effort (0/2/10/30minutes), two buyer rules and four financing/cost regimes. Fourteen predeclared adverse corners add cheap AI, cheap human work, price competition, slow collections, high maintenance, quality shock and dissatisfied buyers to the zero-integration,10-minute,.25-arrival condition. Those corners are selected before their outcomes. Every other source/configuration remains frozen.

The four regimes distinguish full historical cost incurred at opening from prospective adoption-only cost, and earned-surplus adoption funding from seed-funded adoption. Historical construction at opening always uses finite seed resources and is never called self-financed. The seed-funded rule can invest from still-available seed but does not create revenue. Adoption-only rows leave original research sunk; their positive results cannot repay that omitted history. There is no tested successor beyond the same actually built retained or conventional producer.

## Buyer reservation-price identity

Let `U*` be the maximum of zero (defer) and the buyer's utilities for all feasible alternatives. Then the enterprise's strict deterministic reservation-price ceiling is:

`Pmax = qE × V − effortCostE − (1 − qE) × errorLoss − delayCostE − switchingIntegrationCostE − U*`.

A strictly preferred purchase requires `postedPrice < Pmax`, adequate buyer budget and an available provider slot. Equality may tie; the exact code tie rule is alphabetical. Logit can choose another positive-utility nondominated offer, but never a strictly dominated one. This identity explains why a low provider operating cost alone cannot generate demand. It lowers an affordable price floor, while the ceiling depends on usefulness, credible quality, buyer work, trust and practical alternatives.

## A path-specific economic threshold

For each common-draw pair define `Delta = terminalValueB − terminalValueA` after every declared cost. Let `Kcharged` be B's complete one-off capability charge actually incurred, paid or still obligated (including historical cost in the full-history regime). Holding the *realized path* fixed, the maximum one-off charge consistent with `Delta ≥ 0` is `Kmax_path = Delta + Kcharged`. Maintenance, routine operating expense, lost sales and cash effects remain inside Delta.

This is an accounting threshold conditional on that realized path. It is **not** a global cost-optimization result: changing K can alter budget feasibility, adoption timing, the competing price path, buyer choices and failures. The experiment therefore reports actual causal differences and finances separately, and never extrapolates a positive path to all higher/lower construction costs. Negative Kmax means that even a free one-off asset would fail on that fixed path. Actual unknown costs remain unknown outside the model.

Both B and C receive equal source-supported functionality, costs, information and funds. Their equality is a required null result. No equilibrium, algorithmic superiority, actual adoption, collected revenue, original R&D self-funding, second-generation capability or self-acceleration claim is made.
